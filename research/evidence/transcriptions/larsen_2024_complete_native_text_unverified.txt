                                                                                  Abstract
The selective hydrogenation of acetylene to ethylene remains an industrially relevant process used to
remove acetylene impurities from ethylene-rich product streams derived from stream cracking. Palla-
dium based catalysts are widely used for this reaction, but their tendency to over-hydrogenate ethylene
to ethane limits its selectivity. The addition of silver as diluent metal has been shown to improve the
selectivity through both the geometric and electronic effects on the active palladium sites.

   In this work, three different PdAg/SiO2 catalysts with varying palladium loadings (Pd:Ag ratios of
1:200, 1:100, and 1:50) were prepared by incipient wetness co-impregnation and tested for selective
acetylene hydrogenation at 100 °C and 1000 mbar. The effects of increasing flow rate, acetylene, ethy-
lene and hydrogen concentrations were tested. Catalyst characterization was performed using powder
XRD and H2 -TPR.

    The experimental results have shown a trade-off between conversion and selectivity as a function
of palladium loading. The catalysts with higher palladium loading have higher conversion, but also pro-
moted over-hydrogenation, reducing selectivity. Catalysts with lower palladium loading have shown to
maintain higher selectivity, but reduced activity.

    Kinetic modeling using an extended LHHW model was applied to describe the observed catalytic
behavior. The final model includes a maximum rate cap for acetylene hydrogenation and a flexible
acetylene inhibition term for the over-hydrogenation reaction. This models has provided the best fit
for all three catalysts with overall RMSE values of 5.10, 5.43, and 7.14 percentage points for Pd0 Ag,
Pd2 Ag, and Pd3 Ag respectively. The results suggest that acetylene and ethylene hydrogenation are
governed by different physical limitations, and that the Pd2 Ag offers the most favorable balance be-
tween conversion and selectivity.

   Key Words: Acetylene hydrogenation, palladium silver bimetallic catalysts, LHHW kinetic modeling

                                                                            Introduction
A world without ethylene (C2 H4 ) is unthinkable, as it is one of the largest standard chemical building
blocks. Ethylene is widely used as a feedstock for the production of polymers and polymer precursors,
including polyethylene and polyvinyl chloride (PVC). Industrial ethylene is produced mainly through oil
steam cracking, a process that is not free of hurdles. This is due to the resulting ethylene rich product
stream can contain small amounts of acetylene (C2 H2 ). Although acetylene can only be found in the
stream in very small quantity, it still is known to poison to the Ziegler-Natta catalyst used in downstream
ethylene conversion processes.1 Therefore, acetylene must be removed from this product stream be-
fore further processing.


    This is typically done through selective hydrogenation of acetylene to ethylene (C2 H2 + H2 −−−→
C2 H4 ). This route is attractive because it removes acetylene, while possibly increasing the amount of
ethylene in the stream. However, for this selective hydrogenation reaction a problem arises: ethylene
can also hydrogenate to ethane (C2 H4 + H2 −−−→ C2 H6 ). The formation of ethane lowers the ethylene
yield and decreases the value of the purified ethylene stream. Therefore, for this process an effective
catalyst must not only achieve high conversion, but also needs to suppress ethylene hydrogenation.
This balance between acetylene conversion and ethylene selectivity has proven to be the main chal-
lenge in selective acetylene hydrogenation.


    Palladium (Pd) is among the most widely studied and industrially relevant catalysts for acetylene
hydrogenation because of its high hydrogenation activity. Palladium has proven to very efficiently ac-
tivate hydrogen and adsorb unsaturated hydrocarbons. However, this efficiency also causes serious
limitations. If acetylene concentration decreases, more active sites become available for ethylene to
adsorb on. As a result, the catalysts selectivity can drop when high acetylene conversion is achieved.
   In addition to over-hydrogenation, other unwanted side reactions can occur during hydrogenation.
One important side reaction is C-C coupling, which can lead to the formation of so called ”green oil”.
Green oil refers to heavier C4+ hydrocarbon species formed through coupling reactions between ad-
sorbed acetylene- and ethylene-derived intermediates. These C4+ species can accumulate on the
catalyst surface and ultimately lead to deactivation of the catalyst due to blockage of its active sites.2,3
    Coke formation is another side reaction that can occur. Coke is formed through decomposition or
further transformation of strongly adsorbed hydrocarbon species, which again leads to catalyst deac-
tivation.2,4–6 The main reactions and, side reactions, that can occur during this reaction are shown in
Figure 2.1.

    A common strategy used to improve the selectivity of palladium based catalysts is through dilution
with another metal. Metals such as copper (Cu) and silver (Ag) have proven to increase palladium
based catalysts selectivity.7–13 The addition of a second metal can influence the catalyst both geomet-
rically and electronically. Geometrically, a second metal can dilute the catalyst surface and break up
large palladium ensembles, reducing the number of sites that promote over-hydrogenation. Electron-
ically, the second metal can modify the electronic structure of palladium. This can shift the d-band of
palladium, resulting in a decrease of adsorption strength of the catalyst.3,7,14 Through these effects,
diluted palladium catalysts can improve the balance between acetylene conversion and ethylene se-
lectivity.

   Besides the addition of a second metal, the catalyst support also plays an important role. The
support mainly influences the metal dispersion, but can also influence active site accessibility. Com-
monly investigated supports for acetylene hydrogenation are metal oxides such as Al2 O3 , TiO2 and
SiO2 .2,4,7,10,15,16 In this research, SiO2 was used as a support because it is generally considered to be
more inert compared to other supports. This makes it suitable for studying the effect of different Pd/Ag
compositions while minimizing additional support related effects.

    Among bimetallic catalysts, PdAg remains among the most studied and industrially relevant cata-
lysts for this reaction.2,5,17 Previous studies have shown that PdAg catalysts can achieve high conver-
sion, while maintaining relatively high selectivity. However, exact performance depends strongly on
catalyst composition, particle structure, support, and reaction conditions.2 This means that the palla-
dium silver ratio is a crucial design parameter. A too low loading of silver may leave larger palladium
ensembles to promote over-hydrogenation, while a too high loading can dilute the palladium too much
thereby decreasing catalyst activity. Therefore, identifying an optimal palladium silver ratio requires a
systematic comparison of catalytic performance.

    Kinetic modeling is a useful tool for making this comparison more quantitative. Experimental con-
version and selectivity show how a catalyst performs under tested condition, but kinetic modeling can
help relate this performance to reaction rates, adsorption effects, inhibition terms, and reaction orders.
Especially for this reaction, kinetic modeling is useful because it can describe both the desired and un-
desired hydrogenation reactions that can occur at the same time. With kinetic modeling these reaction
can be described independently from each other. The kinetic parameters can provide insight into how
different catalysts promote the desired reaction while suppressing other reactions. In addition, a suffi-
ciently accurate kinetic model can be used to estimate catalyst performance under reaction conditions
that were not tested experimentally.

   The aim of this research is therefore to investigate different PdAg ratios in PdAg/SiO2 catalysts
on the selective semi hydrogenation of acetylene to ethylene. The catalysts are evaluated on their
conversion and selectivity, and kinetic modeling is used to quantify differences in catalytic behavior
between the different compositions. By combining experimental and simulated data, this work aims to

                                                  Literature Background
3.1. Industrial relevance of the semi-hydrogenation reaction
After pumping crude oil, it still needs to undergo some changes before it can be used as fossil fuels
or feedstock for plastics. The crude oil mainly exists of long hydrocarbon chains, which need to be
broken apart to smaller hydrocarbon chains. Breaking these hydrocarbon chains is called the cracking
of crude oil. This cracking happens in a cracking furnace, after which the cracked hydrocarbons go
to a fractional distillation tower and get separated based on boiling points and chain length. However,
this separation is not very precise, meaning that there can be some unwanted by products. One of
these ”over-cracked” by products is acetylene, which stays mixed with the desired ethylene, usually
this stream contains only an acetylene concentration up to 3%.18 Still, concentrations such as these
poison the Ziegler-Natta catalyst used for the polymerization of ethylene, which is the reason it needs
to be reduced to below 5 ppm in the feed stream to obtain higher yields.2 One of the most effective
ways to date remains the catalytic semi-hydrogenation of acetylene. The hydrogenation of acetylene
creates more ethylene and thus improves yield and product quality.19

Polanyi mechanism is described below.

3.2. Current catalysts, and their challenges
For this hydrogenation reaction of acetylene, a lot of different metals have been found to be able to act
as catalysts. The catalysts used for this reaction all work in the same principle, bringing the acetylene
and hydrogen molecules together while activating hydrogen. The working of the catalyst starts with
the adsorption of acetylene on its surface, because of the triple bond of acetylene it can relatively easy
adsorb on the catalysts surface. In the meantime, the hydrogen also gets adsorbed and activated on
the surface. This activation of hydrogen is one of the main reasons why a catalyst is needed for this
reaction. Hydrogen on its own is a stable molecule and therefore is normally quite difficult to react
with. After both these molecules are adsorbed the earlier described Horuiti-Polanyi mechanism can
happen and the newly produced ethylene can desorb. However, this is where the challenge for these
catalysts arise because if the acetylene concentration gets to low ethylene also can get adsorbed by the
catalyst. When ethylene is adsorbed, the same mechanism can occur again leading to the formation
of ethane and giving the low selectivity towards ethylene known for this reaction, and therefore the
biggest challenge at this point in time.

3.2.1. Palladium catalysts
Throughout the years, transition metals like palladium (Pd), gold (Au), nickel (Ni) and copper (Cu)
have been proven useful for this reaction.2 Nickel was one of the first catalysts found to be able to
promote hydrogenation. However, after some time, other transition metals have also been found to
be good catalysts with great conversion and reasonable selectivity towards ethylene. Since the 1950s
monometallic palladium has been the standard industrially, because of its ability to easily activate hydro-
gen and ease in adsorbing acetylene, while also desorbing ethylene relatively well.5 Catalyst existing
of palladium on a carbon support have been found to achieve conversion of 92% and selectivity of
50% at 75 °C.21 While other metals like gold for example achieve a conversion of 80%, while having
a selectivity of 76% at 150 °C on a C-TiO2 -R250 support.22 For this reason, palladium remains an im-
portant catalyst in industrial applications, despite its relatively high cost. This also helps explain why it
continues to be extensively investigated in catalyst research.
     However, even for palladium catalysts, improvements can be made. The main area for improvement
is the catalysts low selectivity when high conversion is achieved. Achieving this higher selectivity can be
achieved by different methods, but the method with the greatest potential has been catalyst modification
by the addition of another metal. This is the area in which most research is done nowadays and is also
showing significant improvements since research has started.

3.2.2. Catalyst dilution
As stated above, the palladium catalyst is continuously improving through catalyst modification, and
the most researched is the addition of another metal. The beneficial effect, on semi-hydrogenation,
of adding a second metal was first discovered in 1952 by Herbert Lindlar, and the catalyst found is
called the Lindlar catalyst. Lindlar added palladium on calcium carbonate (CaCO3 ) after which it was
poisoned by lead (Pb) or sulfur (S).23 Specifically for the selective hydrogenation of acetylene bimetallic
catalysts were introduced in the 1980s.5 All of these catalysts used palladium as the base metal and
promoted it with different metals such as the previously mentioned silver (Ag) and gold (Au), but also
metals such as nickel (Ni), zinc (Zn) or lead (Pb).5 The purpose of diluting palladium with a second metal
is to modify the active sites of palladium geometrically and electronically. This weakens the adsorption
of ethylene on the catalyst surface, allowing ethylene to desorb more easily and thus suppressing its
further hydrogenation to ethane, as can be seen in Figure 3.1.17 Palladium diluted with silver is the
catalyst in which most research has been done and is also the most widely used form of industrial
catalyst for the hydrogenation of acetylene to ethylene.2,5,17 This proven effectiveness industrially is
the reason most research keeps concentrating on improving these palladium-silver catalysts.

Geometric effect
Bimetallic catalysts improve the selectivity of the palladium catalyst in two different ways, the introduc-
tion of the second metal tunes the geometric and electronic structure of the catalyst.12 By changing the
geometric structure, the selectivity of the catalyst is increased because it breaks up large ensembles
of palladium atoms.
    Large ensembles as described above are the main components that promote hydrogenation to-
wards ethane. On these palladium rich ensembles acetylene can strongly bind to the catalyst through
multi-coordinated 𝜋/𝑑𝑖-𝜎 type bonding.10 Besides the ensemble effect affecting acetylene and ethylene
adsorption, it also influences hydrogen activation. Between palladium atoms hydrogen can be moved
or stored which increases the chance of hydrogen being close to an adsorbed acetylene or ethylene
molecule.13 But this is the same effect is where the problem towards selectivity originates from, the
bonds these large ensembles form are so strong the newly formed ethylene can remain adsorbed, or
can get readsorbed further in the reactor. If there is an activated hydrogen atom close to this adsorbed
ethylene the hydrogenation will continue, ultimately forming ethane as an end product.
    Through breaking these large ensembles up, with metals like silver, the possibility of forming such
strong multi-coordinated 𝜋/𝑑𝑖-𝜎 type bond becomes harder. This leads to acetylene only being able to
bond to the catalyst surface through a weaker 𝜋-bond.10 However, the bond is still strong enough for the
conversion of acetylene to ethylene, but the bond is not strong enough for ethylene to remain adsorbed
or be readsorbed further in the reaction, preventing further hydrogenation to ethane. In addition to
this effect, the dilution of palladium in the catalyst also ensures that the chance of a hydrogen atom
being close enough to adsorbed ethylene for further hydrogenation gets smaller.13 This shows that
the ensemble effect has a large influence on the selective hydrogenation reaction by being able to
significantly increase the selectivity of the reaction towards ethylene.

Possible catalyst configurations
This ensemble effect can also be influenced by the way these bimetallic catalysts active sites are config-
ured. The most researched configurations are disordered alloy sites, ordered intermetallic compound
(IMC) phases, and single-atom (SA) sites. These configuration possibilities are schematically shown
in Figure 3.2.




Figure 3.2: Schematic view of distinct types of active sites for acetylene hydrogenation. Adapted from Lan, Chen, and Wang [2]


    In disordered alloy sites, the chance of large palladium ensembles is still significant. These cata-
lysts are commonly prepared through methods such as co-impregnation or sequential impregnation.
After which they are reduced through thermal treatment.17 However, both of these preparation methods
do not include any way of influencing the way both metallic atoms are ordered, leaving us with limited
atomic-level control over the ordering of palladium-silver.24 As a result, isolated palladium atoms, small
palladium ensembles, and large palladium ensembles can co-exist depending on the total palladium
loading.25,26 This explains how with disordered alloy sites, the ensemble effect can still have an influ-
ence on the catalysts performance.

    For intermetallic compounds this is different, the atomic arrangement is more ordered. These cata-
lysts are prepared with controlled metal ratios, after which they undergo a high temperature reduction.
This high temperature reduction allows the atoms to rearrange in an ordered structure.27 The ordered
structure into which these atoms rearrange often has a predefined stoichiometry and crystal structure.28
However, whether the ensemble effect is still at play depends on the specific intermetallic phase and
the surface structure.29 When palladium atoms are still neighboring each other with this structure, the
ensemble effect can still become active, once again having lower selectivity of the catalyst towards
ethylene as consequence.
    The final configuration on which a lot of research has been done is single-atom sites. In these types
of catalysts, palladium atoms are isolated in a sliver rich matrix.30 For this to be possible, very dilute
amounts of palladium are introduced together with silver precursors onto a support by incipient wetness
impregnation. This is then followed by drying and reduction in hydrogen, resulting in a palladium-silver
alloy phase, and when the palladium loading is sufficiently low, palladium atoms can become atomically
dispersed within the silver matrix, preventing the formation of larger palladium ensembles.31 Without
neighboring palladium atoms, the known palladium ensemble effect is severely suppressed, which
therefore prevents the multi-coordinated bond with acetylene or ethylene.8 The danger with SA-sites
is that the palladium load is too low, which ultimately decreases the conversion of acetylene and thus
the catalyst becomes inactive.32

Electronic effect
The other effect described is the change in electronic structure that the addition of a second metal brings
with it. Whenever a metal is combined with another metal, they influence each other on an electronic
level, this electronic level is the d-band of palladium.17 Silver has been found to move an electron from
silver to palladium filling the d-band of palladium.8 The filling of the d-band improves the selectivity of
the reaction because it lowers the d-states to lower energy. And since both acetylene and ethylene bind
through their 𝜋-orbitals with the d-states of palladium, favorable back donation interactions by palladium
are reduced.33,34 This ultimately leads to a weaker interaction between acetylene and ethylene with
palladium, which again reduces over-hydrogenation to ethane, thus improving selectivity.11

3.2.3. Effect of metal-oxide catalyst support
In addition to the composition of the bimetallic catalyst, the metal-oxide support on which it is placed
also influences its selectivity towards hydrogenation of acetylene. The strongest influence of the sup-
port is to stabilize the metal atoms during the reaction. If palladium atoms become mobile, they can
migrate and aggregate into larger ensembles, a phenomenon known as sintering, which especially has
a great influence on the selectivity of a catalyst at higher temperatures.16 Oxide supports prevent this
phenomenon by separating the metal atoms and anchoring them to the support surface.35 First of all, by
adding a support to the active palladium atoms are more spread out than without support, making the
chance of them finding each other during heating smaller.36 Secondly, the anchoring through oxygen
atoms and hydroxyl groups; palladium atoms can coordinate towards the surface oxygen atoms, form-
ing palladium-oxygen bonds. This bond improves adhesion between the active catalyst and the support,
making sintering less favorable, once again preventing the formation of larger palladium ensembles.35
Next, the addition of a catalyst support also helps spread the heat evenly, therefore preventing local hot
spots where sintering or side reactions can take place.15 There are many metal-oxides that can be used
as a support for this specific reaction, among the most common are Al2 O3 , SiO2 , and TiO2 .2,5,15,16,18,20
However, industrially the most used support is Al2 O3 , specifically 𝛼-Al2 O3 supports are very common to
find.2,5,16,20 However, SiO2 has proven its worth in research towards single-atom palladium-silver cat-
alysts because SiO2 is a relatively inert support, therefore making it easier to observe the metal-alloy
effect without support effects.37

3.4.3. Mass Transfer Limitations
However, there is still is a factor that both models do not take into account. Both models assume
the measured reaction rates only reflect the intrinsic catalyst behavior. This assumption can only be
made when mass transfer does not significantly limit the transport of reactants.41 If external, or internal
mass transfer becomes rate-limiting the observed reaction rate can, next to adsorption and surface
reaction steps, also be determined by diffusion of reactants through the gas phase, the pores of the
catalyst or inside the particle.41 Mass transfer limitations can occur in different phases of the reaction,
such as diffusion of the feed stream to the catalyst surface (external) and diffusion in the catalyst
pores (internal).41,42 Typical signs of mass transfer limitations include a significant dependence of the
observed reaction rate on flow rate, and more active catalysts does not result in proportionally higher
reaction rates.42,43
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 19 / 97>
3.4. Kinetic modeling                                                                                    13


External mass transfer
External mass transfer limitations occurs when reactants are consumed at a rate at the catalyst surface
faster than that supplied by the feed stream. In this case, the concentration of the feed stream is higher
than at the catalyst surface.43 Kinetic models such as the LHHW model may assume incorrect reactant
concentrations at the active sites when limited external mass transfer occurs. The model can assume
the concentration of acetylene at the catalyst surface is equal to the concentration in the bulk gas
phase.20 When internal mass-transfer limitations are present, this assumption is no longer valid and
must be corrected.

Internal mass transfer
Internal mass transfer limitations occur when reactants cannot diffuse through the pores of catalyst
particles fast enough to reach all internal active sites. As a result, the whole catalyst particle is not
effectively used. The active sites of the catalyst near the surface are used mainly, while more internal
active sides experience lower reactant concentrations.44

Correcting for mass transfer limitations and other physical effects
When mass transfer limitations are significant, the observed reaction rate no longer only reflects the
intrinsic surface kinetics. In that case, a purely kinetic LHHW model may overpredict the reaction rate,
especially under conditions where the intrinsic acetylene hydrogenation rate becomes very high. To
account for this effect in a simplified way, an empirical rate cap can be introduced for the acetylene
hydrogenation rate. This correction limits the rate predicted by the LHHW expression to a maximum
observable rate, (𝑟1,max ), as shown in equation 3.6.43 It should be noted that this rate cap should not
be interpreted as representing only mass transfer limitations. Instead, it is an empirical correction that
takes the maximal observed rate into account. Therefore is this cap a combination of several effects
as reactor operation and catalyst bed behavior. But mass transfer limitations are expected to have a
significant influence on this maximum observed rate, because of their possible restriction of reactants
reaching the active sites. As a result, when the intrinsic reaction rate is low, 𝑟1 ≈ 𝑟1,𝐿𝐻𝐻𝑊 , the reaction
is kinetically controlled. In contrast, when the intrinsic reaction rate becomes higher 𝑟1 becomes closer
to 𝑟1,𝑚𝑎𝑥 , eventually reaching a maximum reaction rate controlled by other effects, like mass transfer
limitations.

Conversion
In general, increasing the mass-normalized space velocity leads to a decrease in acetylene conversion,
as can be seen in the graph in Figure 6.1(a). This can be explained by the shorter residence time of the
gas over the catalyst surface. At higher flow rates, acetylene molecules have less time to adsorb on
the catalyst surface and react with hydrogen before leaving the reactor. As a result, more unconverted
acetylene remains in the product stream.

    Both Pd0 Ag and Pd2 Ag follow this trend, while the conversion of Pd3 Ag remains close to 100% with
higher flow rates. This is consistent with the higher palladium loading of Pd3 Ag, which likely provides
more available active sites for acetylene hydrogenation. As a result, this catalyst can compensate
better for the shorter residence time. For Pd0 Ag and Pd2 Ag, the lower palladium loading likely results
in fewer active palladium sites. Therefore, these catalysts cannot compensate for the shorter residence
time, leading to a decrease in conversion as the flow rate increases.
    The first order check in Figure 6.1(c) was used to evaluate whether the decrease in conversion
follows first order behavior. For Pd0 Ag, the data shows a clear linear trend, indicating that the con-
version decrease is consistent with first order acetylene consumption. Unfortunately there are only 2
data points for Pd2 Ag, therefore not much can be said about the reaction order. However, first-order
behavior too can be assumed for this catalyst.


Selectivity
In contrast to conversion, ethylene selectivity generally increases with increasing flow rates, as shown
in Figure 6.1(b). The results show that the selectivity for ethylene increases with increasing GHSV.
This trend can be explained by the same residence-time principle used to describe the conversion
results. Newly formed ethylene has less time to readsorb on the catalyst surface and undergo further
hydrogenation to ethane. Therefore, a larger fraction of the converted acetylene leaves the reactor as
ethylene.
     For Pd0 Ag, the ethylene selectivity is already high over the full GHSV range and only slightly in-
creases with increasing flow rate. This indicates that further hydrogenation of ethylene is already largely
suppressed for this catalyst, even at lower flow rates. Therefore, increasing the flow rate has only a
limited additional effect on its selectivity.
     For Pd2 Ag a much stronger increase in selectivity is observed, from ∼55 to ∼99%. This suggests
that for this catalyst, at lower flow rates ethylene hydrogenation is more allowed. But with an increase
in flow rate this problem can be strongly reduced, resulting in a selectivity of almost 100%.
     In contrast, Pd3 Ag shows negative ethylene selectivity at low flow rates. This indicates net ethylene
consumption, meaning that the ethylene from the feed and/or newly formed ethylene is further hydro-
genated to ethane. This behavior is consistent with the higher palladium loading, which makes it more
active for hydrogenation. Even at the highest flow rate, the selectivity remains relatively low compared
with other catalysts, showing that this catalyst is too active for selective ethylene formation under these
conditions.

Conclusion on GHSV effect
Overall, the flow rate experiments show a clear trade-off between acetylene conversion and ethylene
selectivity. Increasing the flow rate decreases the residence time, which can lower acetylene conversion
for certain catalysts. At the same time, increased flow rate prevents ethylene hydrogenation, thereby
improving selectivity. This trade-off has been found to strongly depend on palladium loading. Pd0 Ag
shows the highest selectivity over the full GHSV range, but its lower loading limits its ability to maintain
high conversion at higher flow rates. In contrast, Pd3 Ag maintains a high conversion even as the
flow rate increases, but shows significantly lower ethylene selectivity than the other catalysts due to
stronger overhydrogenation. Pd2 Ag shows the clearest improvement in selectivity with increasing flow
rate, suggesting that its performance is especially sensitive to the residence time.

Shortcomings standard LHHW model
The standard LHHW model has shown that for the Pd0 Ag catalyst it gives a mixed description of the
experimental data. The quality differs strongly between conversion and selectivity. The selectivity is
described well, while the conversion fit is less accurate. At lower and intermediate conversion, the
model over- and under predicts data points indicating that it does not capture the correct dependence
of conversion. At higher experimental conversion, several points are simulated too low, meaning that
the model also struggles to reproduce conditions where the catalyst experimentally reaches high con-
version. Therefore, the standard LHHW model captures the conversion trend only weakly, but it is not
flexible enough to accurately simulate the full conversion behavior. From Figure 6.1(a) the conversion
does decrease with increasing flow rate. This suggests that mass transfer limitations are not limiting
for this catalyst, since mass transfer limited reactions would show little sensitivity to flow rate. Never-
theless, this bad fit suggests that the fitted parameters are not really physically meaningful.

    In contrast, for Pd2 Ag it seems the model describes the catalyst relatively well. Especially acety-
lene conversion is suggested to be simulated quite well, but every simulated conversion is ∼100%.
In contrast, the selectivity fit does not seem so good. At lower and intermediate experimental values
the model tends to overestimate it. This indicates that the standard LHHW model does not accurately
describe the balance between 𝑟1 and 𝑟2 . This balance can come from the same mistake that ensures
every simulated conversion is ∼100%. A possible explanation could be parameter compensation of 𝑘1
and 𝐾1 . It is plausible that these parameters are artificially high to fit most of the experimental ∼100%
results rather than correctly describing the kinetics of the catalyst. This parameter compensation can
also be the reason the ethylene selectivity fit is off. An artificially high 𝑘1 might force the model to-
ward high acetylene conversion, which can indirectly affect the predicted balance between ethylene
formation and ethane formation. Compensation of 𝐾1 increases surface coverage of acetylene, this
can suppress 𝑟2 and increase simulated selectivity. This can explain the overestimation of selectivity at
low experimental selectivity and underestimation at higher experimental selectivity. In Figure 6.1(a) in
the beginning the conversion is not influenced by increasing GHSV, consistent with possible external
mass transfer limitations. However, after a certain point the conversion does drop, which is expected
for a catalyst without physical limitations. Therefore, even though the overall RMSE suggests a rela-
tively well fit, it is very likely that the parameters are physically meaningless and the good fit is artificially
created.

    The LHHW model does significantly show its limitations for the Pd3 Ag catalyst. Although the simu-
lated conversion seems relatively well fit, this is again because the model predicts ∼100% conversion
for every condition. Once again, this too suggests the idea of parameter compensation. The modeled
selectivity values similarly deviate substantially from experiment, showing that the LHHW model re-
quires modification to accurately describe the competition between 𝑟1 and 𝑟2 . For this catalyst Figure
6.1(a) does suggest mass transfer limitations need to be taken into account. In this figure the con-
version of Pd3 Ag does not decrease with increasing GHSV. This behavior is consistent with possible
external mass transfer limitations. However, because the conversion remains close to 100%, this trend
alone is not sufficient to prove mass transfer limitations.

In total, three modifications to the standard LHHW model were evaluated to determine which physical
effects are needed to best describe acetylene hydrogenation over the different PdAg catalysts. The

standard LHHW model includes adsorption terms for acetylene, ethylene and hydrogen in the denom-

not only describe the reaction rate through the rate constants, but also through the surface coverage
of the different adsorbing species.

    The first modifications were made to see whether the LHHW model could be simplified. This was
done by removing the hydrogen terms in the model, evaluating the hydrogen importance in the model.
If the simplified model gave a similar or better fit, this would suggest that the hydrogen terms are
not necessary to describe the data. However, this does not mean hydrogen has no influence on the
reaction.
    Secondly, an inhibition term was added to the rate equation for ethylene hydrogenation. This was
added because acetylene can inhibit the second hydrogenation step by blocking active catalyst sites
through its stronger adsorption on palladium active sites. The addition of this term therefore makes
it possible to describe a situation where ethylene hydrogenation is suppressed by the presence of
acetylene. This can improve the selectivity fit because it suppresses the modeled formation of ethane.

the strength of the inhibition effect at higher acetylene concentrations. Chemically, this can represent
stronger site blocking or a more pronounced suppression of ethylene hydrogenation when the surface
is highly covered by acetylene. This modification can therefore be found useful when the linear inhibi-
tion term is too weak to describe the observed decrease in ethylene hydrogenation.

6.2.4. Cross-catalyst comparison
For every catalyst, the final extended LHHW kinetic model gave the best fit. However, from this one
cannot conclude that every catalyst has the same kinetics because different parameters were fitted for
every catalyst. Using the same final kinetic model allows the fitted parameter sets to be compared,
which gives insight into how the catalysts differ kinetically.

    In this model the rate constants 𝑘1 and 𝑘2 describe their contribution to their representative hydro-
genation reaction. The rate constant 𝑘1 relates to the hydrogenation of acetylene to ethylene, while 𝑘2
relates to the over-hydrogenation of ethylene to ethane. For selectivity, the relative balance between
𝑘1 and 𝑘2 is especially important, while the absolute values also influence the predicted conversion
and overall reaction rate. A relatively large 𝑘1 compared to 𝑘2 indicates that the semi hydrogenation
is favored, resulting in higher ethylene selectivity. In contrast, a stronger contribution of 𝑘2 suggests a
higher tendency for overhydrogenation. This trend can be connected to the ensemble effect. Larger
palladium loading usually leads to larger palladium ensembles which are more active in hydrogenation
because they provide neighboring palladium sites for adsorption and hydrogen activation. However,
these larger ensembles also increase the probability that ethylene stays adsorbed and hydrogenates to
ethane. Therefore, an increase in palladium loading usually coincides with a lower ratio 𝑘1 : 𝑘2 , showing
the increased chance of over-hydrogenation. This can also clearly be seen in the ratio for Pd0 Ag and
Pd3 Ag, explaining the tendency of Pd3 Ag to over hydrogenate and therefore its lower selectivity.

    The fitted adsorption parameters 𝐾C2 H2 , 𝐾C2 H4 and 𝐾H2 describe how these reactants compete for
the catalyst surface. The strongest interaction is between acetylene and the catalyst surface. This can
be explained by its ability of forming such a strong bond with palladium. This results in the adsorption
constant of acetylene being the largest of the three, and therefore more strongly dominates the catalyst
surface. This increases the selectivity because this prevents active sites adsorbing ethylene and over
hydrogenating. The ethylene adsorption constant has the opposite effect. If this parameter increases,
the probability of ethylene readsorption increases, lowering the catalysts selectivity. Therefore, the ratio
between 𝐾C2 H2 and 𝐾C2 H4 contributes to the predicted selectivity, because it describes the extent to
which acetylene can compete with ethylene for surface sites. However, this ratio should be interpreted
together with 𝑘2 , 𝐾H2 , and the inhibition term to be able to describe the difference between the catalysts
quality.
    The hydrogen adsorption constant represents the role of hydrogen availability in the rate expres-
sions. Because hydrogen is needed for both hydrogenation reactions, a higher hydrogen contribution
can increase conversion and may also enhance the second hydrogenation reaction. Therefore, this
constant can promote the conversion of acetylene, but can also decrease selectivity.

   The inhibition parameters 𝐾𝑖𝑛ℎ and 𝑛𝑖𝑛ℎ describe the suppression of ethylene hydrogenation by
acetylene. Both these terms are only included in 𝑟2 to emphasize the competitive adsorption between
acetylene and the other reactants. These terms represents how, when acetylene is adsorbed on the
catalyst surface it strongly inhibits the second hydrogenation reaction.

    For Pd0 Ag, 𝐾𝑖𝑛ℎ is essentially zero; this means that for this final model inhibition has almost no
effect on the catalyst. This suggests that the high selectivity of this catalyst is mainly described by the
low effective contribution of ethylene and the limited hydrogenation capacity of the catalyst. For Pd2 Ag,
this term has a small, but significant value. The value of 𝐾𝑖𝑛ℎ is very small, but through the 𝑛𝑖𝑛ℎ it still
has a significant influence on the model. Through these fitted parameters the balance between acety-
lene and ethylene hydrogenation is better described in the model. The inhibition term has the biggest
influence on Pd3 Ag. This catalyst on its own has a strong tendency to over hydrogenate. The fitted
inhibition contribution helps reduce this over-hydrogenation when acetylene is present. By adding this
term, the model has significantly improved the models selectivity fit, but it still shows that this catalyst
has a high tendency to over hydrogenate.

prevents the model over predicting acetylene conversion to unrealistic values. Without this term, mean-
ingless values would be fitted. This capacity can be influenced by the number of active sites, palladium
loading, but also by the physical limitations when the reaction becomes fast. Therefore, this cap is es-

pecially important for highly active catalysts, the observed conversion may be affected by mass transfer
limitations affecting how efficiently reactants reach the active sites. That is why the fitted rate should be
interpreted as the maximum observable acetylene hydrogenation capacity under the tested conditions,
and not as a directly chemically meaningful parameter.

    Overall, the fitted parameters show how the same final extended LHHW model is able to describe
the kinetic behavior of each catalyst well. For Pd0 Ag, the fitted model mainly reflects a limited con-

Therefore, the parameter comparison shows how the balance between the first and second hydrogena-
tion step changes with palladium loading. However, it is important to keep in mind that all parameters
are fitted simultaneously, and therefore the individual values are not really meaningful. It is mainly the
combination that can be used to compare the catalysts kinetics.

6.2.5. Comparison with experimental data
The same conversion and selectivity analysis as in Chapter 6.1 has been done on the simulated con-
version and selectivity. By reviewing the experimental and simulated conversion and selectivity trends,
the extent to which the kinetic model captures the observed catalytic behavior can be evaluated. In this
section only the influence of GHSV will be discussed and can be found in Figure 6.7. The remaining
plots can be found in Appendix chapters B to D.

     For Pd0 Ag the simulated conversion and selectivity trend follows the experimental values quite well.
The decrease in conversion with increasing GHSV is slightly less pronounced in the simulation, but the
overall trend is reproduced well. The same can be said about the simulated selectivity trend. The
simulation shows the small increase selectivity with increasing GHSV, and in addition starts at a similar
selectivity. Chemically, this suggests that for this catalyst the fitted parameters describe the dominant
kinetic trends reasonably well.
     When comparing the simulated and experimental values of Pd2 Ag it also appears to match well.
The decrease in conversion begins at approximately the same GHSV in both the experimental and
simulated data, indicating that the model captures the general effect of residence time on acetylene
conversion. The selectivity of the simulated data appears to be ∼10% lower than that is calculated
based on the experimental data. This suggests that chemically the model slightly overestimates the
rate of the secondary hydrogenation reaction, or that it underestimates the inhibiting effect of acetylene
on ethylene on active sites. Therefore, although the conversion behavior of Pd2 Ag is captured well,
the difference for selectivity suggests the model does not fully describe the competitive adsorption on
the catalyst surface.
     The results for Pd3 Ag require a careful interpretation. The conversion is almost indistinguishable
between experimental and simulated data. However, as stated before the conversion is ∼100% over
the whole flow rate range. Because the conversion remains close to complete conversion, it provides
limited sensitivity for evaluating the fitted kinetic parameters. In contrast, the selectivity seems to not be
reproduced well. The simulated selectivity for this catalyst starts ∼40% below the experimental value
at that flow rate. This suggests that the model still has difficulty describing the balance between 𝑟1 and
𝑟2 . Therefore, for Pd3 Ag, the final extended LHHW model does not provide a satisfactory description
of the experimental selectivity trend.

                                                                              Conclusion
This research investigated the selective hydrogenation of acetylene in an ethylene rich feed stream over
PdAg/SiO2 catalysts with different palladium loadings (Pd:Ag loadings of 1:200 Pd0 Ag, 1:100 Pd2 Ag,
and 1:50 Pd3 Ag). The main aim was to investigate how different compositions of palladium silver cata-
lysts influence the conversion and selectivity, combining experimental testing with kinetic modeling to
describe the experimentally observed trends.

    The experimental results clearly demonstrate the effect of palladium loading on each catalysts con-
version and selectivity. Higher palladium loading generally shows near complete conversion, which
can be explained by the larger number of active palladium sites, but this is often accompanied with
low selectivity. In contrast, catalysts with lower palladium maintain a higher selectivity, while its load-
ing limited its hydrogenation capacity. The mediate loading of Pd2 Ag emerged as promising catalyst
composition. The catalyst maintained high conversion across most tested conditions, while achieving
significantly better selectivity than catalysts with higher palladium loading.
    The role of silver, as diluting metal, was confirmed by the experimental trends on the catalyst be-
havior. Palladium is necessary for hydrogenation, but large palladium ensembles promote over hydro-
genation too. Through the addition of silver, palladium is diluted and reduces the number of active pal-
ladium sites. As a result, semi hydrogenation can still take place, while the undesired hydrogenation is
suppressed. The observed differences between the catalysts therefore suggest that the interaction be-
tween the palladium loading and silver dilution significantly determines the final catalytic performance.

     The standard LHHW model was not able to correctly describe the experiment data. The model
consistently predicted near complete acetylene conversion regardless of conditions, suggesting that
the fitted parameters were physically meaningless. Furthermore, the standard model too failed to
describe the competition between acetylene and ethylene hydrogenation, leading to a poor selectivity
fit.
     The important improvements made to the standard model are the introduction of a maximum con-
version rate term for acetylene hydrogenation, combined with a flexible inhibition term. The introduction
of a maximum rate prevents the model from over predicting conversion. Instead of parameters com-
pensating for this, the maximum rate term provides a hard limit to the conversion of acetylene. While
the flexible inhibition term also improved the description of the catalysts selectivity. This term gives the
model the freedom to fit the right strength of the inhibition term. This improves the fit because acetylene
acts as not only a reactant but also as an inhibitor of over hydrogenation.

    The final extended LHHW model incorporating both modifications achieved overall RMSE values of
5.10, 5.43 and 7.14 for Pd0 Ag, Pd2 Ag and Pd3 Ag respectively, representing significant improvement.
Especially for Pd2 Ag and Pd3 Ag the additions improved the overall model’s fit.
    The effect this final, improved model has been proven to be catalyst depended. For Pd0 Ag, the im-
provement was relatively limited because the selectivity was already well described by previous models.
In this case, the added flexibility mainly allowed the model to account for conversion limitations without

losing the good selectivity fit.

    Overall this work shows that the performance of PdAg/SiO2 catalysts for acetylene hydrogenation is
strongly affected by palladium loading and silver dilution. Higher palladium loading increases acetylene
conversion, while silver maintains ethylene selectivity by suppressing over hydrogenation. The final ki-
netic model, significantly improved the models fit through the addition of an artificial conversion rate
cap and a flexible inhibition term. This suggests that this model was able to capture the most significant
experimental trends relatively accurate for most catalysts. This suggests that acetylene and ethylene
hydrogenation are affected by different physical limitations; acetylene hydrogenation is mainly limited
by the maximum attainable consumption rate, while ethylene hydrogenation is strongly influenced by
acetylene inhibition. Therefore, the final model provides a useful tool for comparing the catalytic be-
havior and understanding the balance between activity and selectivity in acetylene hydrogenation for
Pd0 Ag and Pd2 Ag, while for Pd3 Ag the model needs to be improvised further before it becomes useful.
